How to check a New York car service before you book

April 30, 2026, updated September 28, 2026

A for-hire trip that starts in New York City needs a TLC-licensed base, vehicle and driver, and all three can be checked in a few minutes. What to look up, what to ask, and what a written quote should list.

Three licenses behind every ride

The Taxi and Limousine Commission licenses for-hire service in New York City at three levels. The base is the company that takes the booking and dispatches the car. The vehicle holds its own TLC license, and so does the driver. The TLC states the rule plainly: if a trip begins in New York City, both the driver and the vehicle must be TLC licensed, or the ride is illegal. Every for-hire vehicle, rideshare cars and limousines included, must also be affiliated with a TLC-licensed base.

Bases come in four classes, community cars (which the TLC also calls liveries), black cars, luxury limousines and high-volume for-hire services, and the class sets how a base may work. Community car, black car and luxury limousine bases all run on prearranged trips. Black car and luxury limousine bases must take more than 90 percent of their business in payments other than cash from the passenger. Community car bases must offer up-front flat fares. Luxury limousine bases run vehicles seating 20 or fewer and may charge garage to garage, on a flat rate, time or mileage, so on an hourly quote it is worth asking whether the clock starts at your door or when the car leaves its garage.

Look up the base on NYC Open Data

The TLC publishes its licensed bases on NYC Open Data, the city's public data portal at data.cityofnewyork.us, in a dataset named Current Bases. Each row gives the base license number, the licensed entity name, a telephone number, the street address and the type of base. Search it for the company's name, or better, for the base license number once the company has given it to you.

Three details are worth comparing. The entity name is the licensed company, which is not always the brand on a booking site, so ask which base dispatches the trip and look for that name. The telephone number should reach the same dispatch you booked with. The type of base should fit the service being sold, since a luxury limousine base and a community car base are licensed for different work. The dataset notes that the most current list sits in a linked TLC report, so a base missing from the table is worth checking there before drawing a conclusion.

Check the car and the driver

Two more TLC datasets cover the vehicle and the person driving it, and both are refreshed daily between 4 and 7 p.m. For Hire Vehicles (FHV) Active lists every TLC-licensed vehicle in good standing, with its DMV plate number, its TLC vehicle license number and expiration date, and the number, name and type of the base it is affiliated with. Searching the plate of the car that arrives shows which base it answers to. For Hire Vehicles (FHV) Active Drivers lists every licensed driver in good standing, with license number, name and expiration date. The TLC asks users to confirm that the last update date on either dataset reads today or yesterday before relying on it.

At the curb the same checks take seconds. According to the TLC, T&LC is embossed at the bottom of a licensed vehicle's plate, every TLC vehicle should carry a decal showing its TLC license number and expiration, and every driver should have a TLC license on display. That license takes work to get. A new applicant completes a drug test, fingerprinting, a DMV-certified defensive driving course, a TLC course and exam, passenger assistance and wheelchair accessible vehicle training, and a medical certification.

Where illegal rides find passengers

The TLC lists the places unlicensed drivers look for passengers: airports, train stations, concert venues, sporting events, tourist attractions, Broadway shows and hotels. TLC drivers are prohibited from asking people whether they need a ride, and apart from yellow and green cabs every trip must be prearranged through a licensed app or dispatcher. Someone who walks up in an arrivals hall and offers a car is not the car you booked, whatever name they give.

JFK gives arriving passengers the same advice, to use the official pickup areas and to check the car's license plate before getting in. A booked chauffeur should be able to give you the plate, the vehicle and the pickup spot in advance, and the three together make a wrong car obvious. Illegal pickups and other violations by TLC-licensed vehicles can be reported by calling 311 or at portal.311.nyc.gov.

Questions to ask before you pay

A handful of questions separate a careful operator from a vague one, and every answer should be something you can later find in writing.

  • Which base dispatches the trip, and what is its TLC base license number?
  • Is the price a flat fare, an hourly rate, or time and mileage? For hourly work, what is the minimum, and does the clock start at your door or at the garage?
  • How many passengers, and how many large and small bags, does the vehicle class take?
  • How much waiting is included at an airport and at other pickups, is it counted from touchdown or from the booked time, and what does each extra period cost?
  • Where exactly is the airport meeting point, and is a meet inside the terminal with a name sign a separate charge?
  • What happens if the flight lands early, late or at a different airport?
  • What are the cancellation terms, and until when can the trip be changed without charge?

What a written quote should list

A quote worth holding someone to names the vehicle class, the pickup date, time and address, the destination or the booked hours, the flight number on an airport trip, and the base fare. It then lists every charge that sits on top of that fare, or states in writing that the charge is excluded. On a New York City trip these are the ones to look for.

  • Gratuity, either a stated percentage or left to you.
  • Tolls, which depend on the bridges and tunnels the route uses.
  • The New York State congestion surcharge of $2.75 on for-hire trips within the state that begin in, end in or pass through Manhattan south of 96th Street. State rules require it to be passed to the passenger and shown separately on any receipt.
  • The MTA Congestion Relief Zone charge of $0.75 per trip in a black car, paid by the passenger on trips to, from, within or through Manhattan at and below 60th Street.
  • The Black Car Fund surcharge, a small percentage of the fare that member bases collect on every dispatched trip to pay for drivers' workers' compensation and benefits. It is charged on the fare alone, not on tips, tolls or congestion charges.
  • Airport parking, meet and greet fees, waiting time beyond the included window, and taxes.

Get the confirmation in writing, with the total or the method for reaching it, and keep it until the invoice arrives. Key points

  • A trip that starts in New York City needs a TLC-licensed base, vehicle and driver, and all three can be looked up on NYC Open Data
  • At the curb, look for T&LC on the plate, a TLC decal with license number and expiration, and the driver's TLC license on display
  • A written quote should name the vehicle class and fare basis and list every charge on top, including gratuity, tolls, the $2.75 state surcharge and the $0.75 congestion charge
