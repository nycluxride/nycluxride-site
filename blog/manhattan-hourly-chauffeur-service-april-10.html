Hourly or point to point in Manhattan: how to decide

April 10, 2026, updated September 28, 2026

How an hourly block is billed by vehicle class, why the car waits nearby instead of at the door, and the charges that apply below 60th Street.

How three hours are billed by class

Hourly work in Manhattan is sold as a block of time. An hourly booking is billed for at least three hours, so compare the whole block with the one way fares. Current hourly rates by class are on the rates page, and the terms are set out under NYC chauffeur service.

Against that, a single run to an airport is one flat rate to JFK, Newark, Teterboro or LaGuardia, so a day with one trip in it does not belong on the clock. The case for hourly starts at three stops on the island, or at one appointment with no fixed end.

What the block buys is continuity. With separate rides, each leg is a new request, a new car and a new pickup at a curb that may not allow one. On an hourly booking one vehicle and one chauffeur stay assigned for the whole reservation, the bags stay in the trunk, and a meeting that runs forty minutes long starts no second fare. The class decides the seats and the luggage, and capacity for each of the twelve vehicles is listed by class.

Why the car waits nearby, not at the door

Manhattan curb rules are the main reason a held car is worth paying for. Under New York City Department of Transportation rules, waiting is not allowed in a No Stopping, No Standing or No Parking zone. A car may stop to pick up or drop off passengers in a No Standing or No Parking zone, but not in a No Stopping zone, and double parking is illegal at all times, whatever the purpose or the duration.

Idling is limited too. The city bans idling for more than three minutes, or for more than one minute beside a school or a park, and its rules do not count a heater or air conditioner run for cabin comfort as an exemption.

Put together, no car can sit at a Midtown curb for the two hours a meeting takes. On an hourly booking the chauffeur holds the car within reach and brings it back to a named door when called, which is why a short message ten minutes before walking out is the most useful habit on an hourly day. The door matters as much as the address. Park Avenue between 40th and 46th Streets runs on a viaduct around Grand Central Terminal with street-level service roads alongside it from 40th to 42nd Street, and near the Stock Exchange the blocks sit behind barriers, so a corner on William Street, Water Street or Broadway works better. Street by street detail for the rest of the island is under Manhattan pickups.

Charges below 60th Street and below 96th Street

Two public charges attach to for-hire trips in Manhattan, and both are counted per trip. The MTA's Congestion Relief Zone covers local streets and avenues at and below 60th Street, excluding the FDR Drive, the West Side Highway and the Hugh L. Carey Tunnel connections to West Street. Private cars pay a daily toll to enter it. Passengers in taxis and black cars pay a per trip charge instead, $0.75 as of September 2026, and passengers in high-volume app vehicles pay $1.50.

New York State adds a congestion surcharge of $2.75 to each for-hire trip that begins in, ends in or passes through Manhattan south of 96th Street, as long as the trip begins and ends in New York State. It has to be passed to the passenger and shown as its own line on the receipt. A run from Midtown to Newark ends in New Jersey, so the state surcharge does not apply to it.

Because both charges attach to each trip, they multiply with the number of rides. Four separate app rides inside the zone carry $17 in these two charges alone, before any fare. How they are written on an hourly reservation, which covers several legs inside one block of time, is worth asking at booking.

Three Manhattan days, written out

A Midtown meeting day

A pickup on the Upper East Side at 8:30, a first meeting in the office corridor along Park Avenue in the fifties, a second near Grand Central at 42nd Street and Park, lunch, then an afternoon at the Javits Center on Eleventh Avenue north of 34th Street. Every leg is short, and the Javits run crosses town at a slow hour. As separate rides that is five requests and five curbs, each with its own wait. On an hourly Business SUV it is one reservation of about eight hours, and the car comes back to the Javits when the last session ends.

An appointment on York Avenue

Between roughly 66th and 71st Streets, York Avenue holds NewYork-Presbyterian and Weill Cornell Medicine, Memorial Sloan Kettering and Rockefeller University, each with its own driveway or entrance rather than a simple street door. A consultation with no fixed end, a wait at the pharmacy and a ride home is the clearest case for hourly, because nobody has to guess when to call a second car. Give the building name and the department, which is what finds the right door on the Upper East Side.

Dinner in SoHo, then the theater

A table on Spring Street, a show in the theater blocks between 41st and 54th Streets, then a late drink downtown. In SoHo the cast-iron streets, Greene, Mercer, Wooster and Crosby, are one lane wide, so the pickup is a corner on West Broadway, Sixth Avenue, Lafayette Street or Houston Street. The theater blocks empty quickly once the curtain comes down, and a car already assigned to the evening turns that moment into a short walk to an agreed corner rather than a wait with the rest of the crowd.

The Financial District has its own version of the same day: a morning at Broad Street, a meeting uptown on Park Avenue, then dinner back downtown on Stone Street, which is too narrow for a car to wait in.

The days Midtown locks up

NYC DOT names Gridlock Alert Days each year, the days it expects traffic to be at its slowest, and asks people to walk, cycle or take transit where they can. The 2026 list is September 21 to 25, November 18, 19 and 24, and December 2 to 4, 8 to 11, 15 to 18 and 23. On those days a one-way ride is slower to arrive as well as slower to travel.

App prices move with the same pressure. Uber describes surge pricing as rising when ride requests outnumber drivers, with bad weather, rush hour and special events as typical causes, and as specific to areas of a city, so one neighborhood can surge while the next does not. A published hourly rate carries no surge multiplier, so the cost of a gridlocked December afternoon is known before it starts.

When point to point is still the answer

One trip with one destination is a flat rate or a single ride, not an hourly block. Two appointments most of a day apart are the one case to price both ways, because an hourly booking bills the idle middle. A day that might end early still bills the minimum, so one that wraps up after two hours is billed as three. A day that might run late can usually be extended while it is still running, but the limit is whatever assignment is already booked behind yours on that vehicle. A message to dispatch an hour out leaves room to hold the car, and one ten minutes before the scheduled finish may not.

Key points

  • An hourly booking bills at least three hours in every vehicle class, before taxes, tolls and gratuity
  • Manhattan curb rules allow a stop to pick up or drop off but not a wait, so a held car stays nearby until called
  • Every for-hire trip below 60th Street carries a per trip congestion charge, and an in-state trip below 96th Street a $2.75 state surcharge
